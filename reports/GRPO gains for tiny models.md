# Fix the parent, the pool, the step size

The literature does not support expecting a *large* GRPO gain over a fresh-data, compute-matched SFT control at 43M parameters, but it does explain why the last run lost and how to make a real, if modest, win possible. Four things went wrong at once. First, the parent was undertrained, so a continued-SFT control was still climbing on supervised signal. Second, about 80% of rollouts landed in all-correct or all-wrong groups that carry no gradient. Third, the learning rate of 1e-5 is 5–10x above every published RL recipe, including the two from-scratch studies at 45M and 100M parameters. Fourth, std-normalized advantages, a KL of 0.04 and two minibatches per rollout each push in the wrong direction for a near-deterministic policy. The evidence-backed redesign has four parts. Train the SFT parent to a validation plateau before any RL. Stream fresh prompts through a pass-rate filter so that nearly every group is mixed, and weight the stream toward depth 2+. Run on-policy Dr. GRPO-style updates at LR 1e-6 to 3e-6 with G=16 and at least 1,024 samples per step. Compare against both a rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT) control matched on rollouts and an oracle continued-SFT control matched on FLOPs. Realistic expectations from the closest analogues are a few points over the parent on edge-of-competence shapes and less over the oracle-SFT control. A pre-registration should be powered to detect ~2 points with a paired test across at least 3–5 training seeds, not to confirm a large effect.

## The failed run broke four published defaults at once

The learning rate is the single largest deviation. Across 0.5B–32B recipes, RL learning rates sit between 5e-7 and 2e-6, constant, with a short warmup ([DAPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476); [Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783); [ProRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24864)). The two studies closest to MicroReason did not raise it for small models. The **100M from-scratch arithmetic study used 1e-6** with KL 1e-3, temperature 1.0 and global batch 1,024 ([Zhang et al., arXiv 2512.07783](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)). The **45M from-scratch GRPO study used 3e-6**, batch 64, 8 samples, KL 0.005 and only 60 steps ([arXiv 2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)). No source found any sub-100M LR sweep, so 1e-5 is not provably wrong, but nothing supports it.

The advantage normalization amplified noise exactly where the policy is most certain. Dr. GRPO shows that dividing by the group reward std overweights prompts whose rewards are nearly uniform ([Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783)). With 0/1 rewards and G=8, a 1-of-8 group has std of about 0.33, so its advantages are scaled up roughly 3x relative to a balanced group. "Tricks or Traps" found removing the per-group std helps when rewards are concentrated, and that **group mean with batch-level std was the most robust choice** ([Tricks or Traps](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08221)). ScaleRL found prompt-level, batch-level and no std performed similarly at 8B and adopted batch-level ([ScaleRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786)). MicroReason's bimodal pass-rate distribution is the concentrated-reward case.

The KL coefficient of 0.04 is DeepSeekMath's 7B value ([DeepSeekMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03300)). Most later recipes use 0, and recipes that start from an SFT checkpoint use 1e-4 to 1e-3 ([SimpleRL-Zoo](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18892); [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)). Skywork-OR1 found that **even KL 1e-3 pulled the policy back to the reference and stalled gains** ([Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312)). Cui et al. found reference KL stabilized entropy but degraded performance ([Entropy Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22617)). At 40x the from-scratch study's value, the old KL likely anchored the policy to its parent.

Two minibatches per rollout made the updates partly off-policy. Skywork-OR1 measured directly that **more SGD steps per rollout batch speed up entropy collapse and stop test improvement**, and traced it to off-policy data itself ([Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312)). For a policy that is already near-deterministic per prompt, faster collapse is the last thing it needs. With one update per batch, the clipped objective reduces to the plain policy gradient ([TRL docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/grpo_trainer)). At that point GRPO, DAPO, GSPO and CISPO become the same REINFORCE-with-baseline estimator, and the remaining design choices are the baseline, the normalization and the loss aggregation.

Finally, 32 prompts x 8 samples gives 256 samples per step, a quarter of the 1,024 used at 100M ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)). With only ~20% of groups mixed, dynamic sampling had to work hard just to fill a small batch.

## An undertrained parent hands the SFT control a free win

The 6.3-point loss to continued SFT matters more than the 1.8-point loss to the parent, and the literature explains it. RLVR mostly raises pass@1 by moving probability onto solutions the model already finds at larger k, while large-k coverage stays flat or shrinks ([Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13837v5)). In the AIME24 analysis, **0.0% of problems were solved only by the RL model** at k=1024 ([Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13837v5)). Distillation, by contrast, can add new reasoning patterns ([Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837)). "RL Squeezes, SFT Expands" describes the same split: RL prunes incorrect paths and SFT adds correct ones ([arXiv 2509.21128](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21128)). A continued-SFT control fed fresh gold traces is unlimited distillation from a perfect teacher. When the parent saw only 100k examples in about 2 minutes, that control is still in its steep learning phase. RL, a sharpening method, is being raced against an expansion method that has not converged.

The design implication is an inference, not a sourced result, but it follows directly. **Train the parent with SFT until held-out validation accuracy plateaus, then branch RL and the controls from that converged checkpoint.** Measure the plateau by pre-registered criteria, for example less than 0.5 points of held-out-val gain over the last doubling of examples. This makes the RL-vs-control comparison test what RL can add once supervised signal is exhausted. It is also the regime where the 45M study found real GRPO gains: SFT gives partial competence and GRPO then adds +6 to +10 points on compositions ([arXiv 2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)). Do not overshoot into memorization. Chu et al. found RL could not recover out-of-distribution performance from an overfitted SFT checkpoint ([Chu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17161v2)), and GRPO on an already-saturated 98% task did nothing ([arXiv 2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)). Fresh, unlimited synthetic traces make template overfitting unlikely, so converging the parent is low-risk.

A stronger parent cuts both ways. RFT gains fell from about +6 points at 7B–13B to −0.1 at 33B as the SFT model got stronger and produced fewer distinct correct paths ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.01825)). A converged parent will leave less headroom over itself, but the competition with the control becomes fair. Measure the headroom before committing compute. In the gate pool, pass@1 is about 0.66 and pass@8 about 0.76. RL mostly converts within-support mass into greedy correctness, so that gap is a loose ceiling. Recompute it on the converged parent and on the held-out shapes. If pass@16 minus pass@1 on held-out shapes is below about 5 points, a 2-point win is unlikely, and the pre-registration should say so in advance.

## Make every rollout informative by filtering a fresh prompt stream

Prompt selection is the best-supported lever, and MicroReason's unlimited generator makes it nearly free. Every major recipe drops prompts the policy always solves, and most drop those it never solves ([POLARIS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris); [Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312v2)). The cleanest controlled comparison comes from a Qwen2.5-3B SFT model. Plain GRPO averaged 26.3% over five benchmarks. **Filtering to (0,1) gave 27.3%, and a (0.3,0.7) band exceeded 30%**, with up to +10 points on AIME. Online filtering beat offline filtering, and **skewed filters that dropped only easy or only hard prompts did worse than no filter** ([Online Difficulty Filtering, arXiv 2504.03380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03380v2)). POLARIS adds a caution: removing every prompt above a 4/8 pass rate hurt training, and a balanced, slightly hard-skewed mix worked best ([POLARIS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris)). Xiong et al. found most of GRPO's edge over rejection-sampling fine-tuning came from dropping groups with no signal ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11343)).

The recommended design is a streaming filter. Generate fresh prompts, score each at G=16 from the current policy, and keep those with 2/16 to 14/16 correct (roughly a 0.1–0.9 band). Use those same rollouts as the training group, so scoring costs nothing extra. Because prompts never repeat, there is no stale pass-rate problem. With the current distribution of 56% all-correct, 24% all-wrong and 19.5% mixed at G=8, a G=16 filter should pass roughly a quarter to a third of candidates, so filling 64 prompts needs about 200–250 candidates per step. At 43M parameters and ~76-token traces, that is cheap. This replaces the old 0.20 mixed-group gate. The new gate is whether the filter can fill the batch within a pre-registered rollout budget.

Weight the candidate stream by depth. RL produces real capability gains at the "edge of competence," where the model fails pass@1 but solves pass@k ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)). In the 45M study, **GRPO on 2-step data improved 3-step accuracy while GRPO on 1-step data did not**, and nothing extrapolated to depths never seen in training ([arXiv 2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)). The f(g(x)) study found the same at 8B: RL on level-2 compositions lifted level 3 from about 5% to about 30%, while level-1 RL did nothing ([Yuan et al., arXiv 2509.25123](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25123v3)). Theory on single-layer transformers adds a constraint. Policy gradient learns step-by-step algorithms only when the distribution keeps enough mass on simpler examples ([arXiv 2601.15158](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15158v2)). A reasonable pre-registered mix draws about 70% of candidates from 4–6 leaves and harder linear equations, and about 30% from 2–3 leaves. The pass-rate filter then removes whatever is saturated.

Larger groups mainly rescue the hard tail. The probability that a group is mixed is 1 − p^G − (1−p)^G. For a prompt at p=0.05 that is 34% at G=8, 56% at G=16 and 81% at G=32. ScaleRL found 8 vs 32 generations per prompt nearly equivalent at a fixed total batch ([ScaleRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786)), and Skywork-OR1 saw no entropy difference across G=4/8/16 ([Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312v2)). G=16 with a total of at least 1,024 samples per step is therefore the sensible default. Prompts at p≈0 cannot be rescued by any G: under KL-regularized RL the post-training success is f(Q)=aQ/(1−Q+aQ), which is zero when Q is zero ([arXiv 2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)).

Temperature is a secondary lever. Skywork-OR1 saw entropy collapse within about 100 steps at temperature 0.6 and stronger learning at 1.0 ([Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312)). POLARIS chose the highest temperature before quality collapsed, 1.4 for Qwen3-4B ([POLARIS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris)). MicroReason's own attempts moved the mixed fraction only from 0.182 to 0.195 when temperature rose to 1.0, which fits a policy that is confident per prompt. Keep 1.0 and log the distinct-trace ratio. Allow a pre-registered, val-only sweep of {1.0, 1.1, 1.2}, but expect filtering, not temperature, to carry the batch.

## Recommended configuration for the confirmatory arm

Once updates are on-policy, the algorithm reduces to choices about the baseline, normalization and aggregation. Each choice below follows the cited evidence. Every value is extrapolated from 45M–32B results and has not been validated at 43M.

| Setting | Failed run | Recommended | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFT parent | 100k examples, ~2 min | Train to held-out-val plateau | Inference from [Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13837v5), [2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1) |
| Learning rate | 1e-5 | 2e-6 constant, 10–20 step warmup; val-only pilot over {1e-6, 3e-6} | [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783), [2510.15990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1) |
| Updates per rollout | 2 minibatches | 1 (fully on-policy); if 2–4, add eps_high 0.28–0.3 | [Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312), [Tricks or Traps](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08221) |
| Advantage | group mean / group std | group mean (or RLOO) / batch std, or no std | [Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783), [Tricks or Traps](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08221) |
| Loss aggregation | sequence mean | constant denominator (Dr. GRPO); mask truncated samples | [Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.20783), [DAPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476) |
| KL | 0.04 | 1e-3 (k3 estimator) in main arm; KL=0 as pre-registered secondary | [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783), [Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312) |
| Group size / batch | 8 x 32 = 256 | 16 x 64 = 1,024 | [ScaleRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786), [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783) |
| Prompt selection | dynamic sampling on raw pool | streaming filter, 2/16–14/16, depth-weighted | [2504.03380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03380v2), [POLARIS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris) |
| Temperature | — | 1.0; optional val sweep up to 1.2 | [Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312) |
| Entropy | — | no fixed bonus; adaptive floor only if entropy falls below target | [Skywork-OR1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312), [Entropy Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22617) |
| Steps | 300 | 300–500, checkpoint chosen on held-out val by fixed rule | [Entropy Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22617) |

Two monitoring rules should be pre-registered. Gains arrive early: in Cui et al., **about 76% of the performance gain came in the first 200 of 2,400 steps**, tracking entropy loss ([Entropy Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22617)). Log policy entropy and the filter's pass fraction each step. If held-out val is flat after 200 steps, stop and record the result rather than extend. Second, compute log-probs for the loss in FP32 and check that on-policy importance ratios sit at 1.0. ScaleRL found an FP32 LM head raised the asymptotic reward from about 0.52 to 0.61 by removing a train/inference mismatch ([ScaleRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786)). If rollouts come from a separate bf16 KV-cache sampler, a silent mismatch would bias every update. An entropy bonus is not recommended: a 0.01 coefficient made entropy explode, and 0.005 stabilized it without beating baselines ([Entropy Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22617)).

### A bounded step reward is a secondary arm, not the main fix

MicroReason's traces are exactly checkable, which removes the main risk of learned process reward models. The published gains are mostly in sample efficiency, not final accuracy. Progress-based dense rewards gave **5–6x sample efficiency and more than 6% accuracy over outcome reward models** in online RL ([Setlur et al., arXiv 2410.08146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08146)). Gao et al. found learned dense rewards "may NOT help or even hurt" next to the success reward, and that policies exploit step rewards by repeating correct but unnecessary steps unless the dense reward is clipped ([Gao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15115)). The only near-scale evidence is the 100M study, where a process-verification reward mix gave **+4–5% pass@1 on the hardest out-of-distribution band** ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)).

If a step-reward arm is included, use r = 1.0·[final answer exact] + λ·(correct-prefix fraction), with λ = 0.2. The prefix fraction counts consecutive reductions before the first error that each shrink the tree by exactly one operator, divided by the gold reduction count. This is bounded in [0,1], cannot be inflated by padding, and guarantees any correct answer outscores any wrong one. Do not use numeric closeness: no primary source supports it, and in multi-step arithmetic a single early sign error produces large numeric error while a guess can land close. Under group normalization the step term fully drives the advantage in all-wrong groups. That is why it rescues the 24% of all-wrong prompts, and also why it needs a guard. Pre-register that deep-expression final accuracy must rise alongside prefix credit, and halt if mean trace length or reductions per trace drift from gold.

## Two controls and a paired, multi-seed test decide the claim

No surveyed paper matched RL and SFT on FLOPs. Most match rollouts, steps or wall time, and wall-time matching favors SFT because RL spends most of its time generating ([Havrilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04642); [Chu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17161v2)). The pre-registration should name one primary unit, total FLOPs (about 2N per generated token plus 6N per trained token), and report wall time and examples seen as secondaries. It also needs two controls that answer different questions. The **primary matched control is RFT/expert iteration**: same prompt stream, same rollouts, fine-tune on the policy's own correct samples. This isolates "outcome RL vs on-policy SFT," the comparison the literature actually contests. In those comparisons expert iteration matched or beat PPO on GSM8K ([Havrilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04642)), and **RAFT++ landed within 0.2–0.8 points of GRPO** ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11343)). The **oracle continued-SFT control** on fresh gold traces, FLOP-matched, is the harder upper-bound comparison, reported as such. A hybrid arm (continued SFT, then GRPO, at the same total budget) is the most winnable framing. The 100M study found mid-training plus light RL beat RL alone by +10.8% out of distribution ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07783)).

Effect sizes should be expected to be small. Over strong SFT models on math, reported greedy gains are roughly +2 to +7 points, and they shrink as SFT strengthens ([Havrilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04642); [Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.01825)). A careful re-evaluation found most claimed 10–15% RL gains for 1.5B models were not significant, with SFT generalizing more consistently ([Hochlehnert et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07086v2)). Several published post-RL models were worse than correctly evaluated baselines ([Chandak et al.](https://safe-lip-9a8.notion.site/Incorrect-Baseline-Evaluations-Call-into-Question-Recent-LLM-RL-Claims-2012f1fbf0ee8094ab8ded1953c15a37)). Evaluate every arm with the same greedy decoder, parser and checkpoint-selection rule.

Evaluation noise is not the binding constraint. With ~6,000 test prompts and paired greedy outcomes, the per-prompt difference has variance of about d − Δ², where d is the discordant rate. Using the standard error formulas in [Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1), that gives:

| Discordant rate d | SE of paired Δ | 95% CI half-width | MDE at 80% power |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.41 pts | ±0.80 | 1.15 pts |
| 0.20 | 0.58 pts | ±1.13 | 1.62 pts |
| 0.30 | 0.71 pts | ±1.39 | 1.98 pts |

An unpaired comparison at p≈0.6 has an MDE near 2.5 points, so pairing is essential. Two other sources of variance dominate. Clustering by expression shape can inflate standard errors up to about 3x ([Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)), so use a cluster bootstrap over held-out shapes and pre-register the shape count. Training-seed variance is not captured by any prompt-level test, and across-seed SDs of several points are common in RL evaluations ([Hochlehnert et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07086v2)). Run at least 3, preferably 5, seeds per arm. Report each seed's paired Δ and a hierarchical bootstrap over seeds and then prompts, and require that every seed, or at least 4 of 5, clears a CI lower bound above zero against the RFT control. Avoid a rule like "point estimate ≥ +2.0": with a true gain of exactly 2, it passes only about half the time. Instead, pair "CI lower bound > 0" with a reported effect size, or test one-sided against 2 points and accept that this needs a true gain near 3. Interval reporting and robust aggregates, rather than point estimates, are the standard remedy ([Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)). Seed SD at 43M is unknown, so estimate it from the pilot before fixing the seed count.

## Conclusion

The failed run does not show that RL cannot help a 43M arithmetic model. It shows that RL from an undertrained parent, on a pool where four-fifths of rollouts carry no gradient, at ten times the usual step size, loses to an oracle teacher that was still teaching. The stronger finding is about the question itself. RL's documented mechanism is sharpening within existing support, and the fresh-gold-trace control is the one baseline that can keep expanding support, so "large and reliable over continued SFT" is the wrong target. The winnable claims are narrower: GRPO beats RFT at matched rollouts, or SFT-then-GRPO beats SFT alone at matched FLOPs, on edge-of-competence depths where the converged parent's pass@k exceeds its pass@1.

The cheapest decisive step comes before any confirmatory GPU spend. Train the parent to plateau and measure pass@16 − pass@1 by depth on the held-out shapes. If that gap is under about 5 points, no hyperparameter choice is likely to deliver a 2-point win, and the pre-registration should record that as the expected null. If the gap is larger, the configuration above gives the experiment its best published chance. The open question that no source answers is how much of that gap RL recovers at this scale. That number, estimated with a seed-level interval, is the result worth publishing either way.
